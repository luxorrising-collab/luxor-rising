# Brand voice — Luxor Rising Insider's Guide

Distilled from the one published article,
`content/articles/valley-of-the-kings-which-tombs/index.mdoc`. When in doubt,
re-read that file — it is the reference recording of the voice.

## Who is speaking
A private concierge in Luxor who actually lives there. Two named people recur:
- **Ahmed** — born on the west bank, twenty minutes from the Valley gate. The
  on-the-ground local authority. Used for the "I've been inside all of them"
  kind of knowledge and for the quiet, dry one-liners.
- **Dr. Nour** — licensed Egyptologist, fact-checks every article. Used to
  anchor factual authority.
- **"Luxor Rising"** — the brand/we voice for most articles.

## The five rules

1. **Simple and direct.** Short words, short sentences, plain structure. You are
   explaining something to a smart friend over tea, not writing a brochure.

2. **Honest to the point of costing us money.** The signature move. Say the thing
   the guidebooks won't:
   > "And this is the honest part that costs us money to say: *Tutankhamun's tomb
   > is, architecturally, one of the least impressive in the Valley.*"
   This candour is what makes everything else believable — and it sells, because
   trust sells.

3. **Specific, always.** Real numbers, real names, real times. Not "early" —
   "at opening, around 6am." Not "it gets hot" — "thirty-eight degrees in the
   ravine." Not "famous treasures" — "the full five-thousand-piece collection …
   at the Grand Egyptian Museum." Specificity is the whole product.

4. **Sensory and concrete, never purple.** "the astronomical ceiling still
   holding its blue"; "a queue with paintings"; "the stone goes orange." One
   vivid image beats five adjectives. Avoid stacked marketing words
   ("luxurious, unforgettable, magical") — they're banned.

5. **The sell is structural, not stated.** We never say "book us because we're
   great." We show the gap:
   > "It is not that our guests get better tombs — anyone can buy the same
   > ticket. It is that they get them *empty*."
   The reader concludes, on their own, that arranging it properly needs someone
   local. That's the close.

## Devices that are "ours"
- **The blockquote as Ahmed's voice** — a short, dry, human line, attributed:
  > People come out of Tutankhamun's tomb slightly embarrassed, as though they
  > were supposed to feel something and did not.
  > — Ahmed, on the west bank
- **The second-person "you"** throughout. The reader is *in* the morning.
- **The soft turn to us** at the end of a section, never the start:
  "This is the single reason we build days the way we do."
- **Italics for the one word that carries the point** (*empty*, *knowing*).
- **"Questions we get asked"** as the FAQ heading (not "FAQ").

## Tone calibration
- Warm but unsentimental. Confident, not boastful. Dry humour, used sparingly.
- Respectful of the place and its people. Egypt is home, not a backdrop.
- British English: colour, metres, favourite, organise.

## What to avoid
- Generic travel-blog filler ("nestled in the heart of", "a hidden gem",
  "bucket list", "must-see", "without further ado").
- Hype adjectives with nothing under them.
- Fake urgency or pressure.
- Claiming anything we can't stand behind. If we'd be embarrassed to say it to a
  guest's face, it doesn't go in.
